Observer

You stand at the center of a flat disk of Earth, inside the celestial sphere. Changing latitude tilts the sphere: the celestial pole sits as high above the horizon as your latitude.

Date and time

Drag the Sun around the ecliptic to change the date.
Times are local solar time. Noon is when the Sun is near due south (or due north, south of the Tropics).

Measure

Click anywhere on the sky to mark a point and read its altitude and azimuth. The mark stays on the sky as time runs.

About this app

This is the Celestial Sphere Explorer seen from where we really stand: at the center of the sphere, on the ground. The stars, constellations, Flamsteed's plates and deep-sky objects are the same data, from Hipparcos and the open d3-celestial data set by Olaf Frohn.

The sky turns once every sidereal day (23 h 56 m). The Sun's place on the ecliptic comes from a standard low-precision formula, good to about a hundredth of a degree. Star positions are for the year 2000 and ignore precession, so over a few decades they drift by a fraction of a degree. Atmospheric refraction, which lifts objects near the horizon by about half a degree, is not included.

Azimuth is measured from north through east (north 0°, east 90°, south 180°, west 270°). Altitude is the angle above the horizon.
