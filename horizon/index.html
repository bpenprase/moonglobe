Observer

You stand at the center of a flat disk of Earth, inside the celestial sphere. Changing latitude tilts the sphere: the celestial pole sits as high above the horizon as your latitude. Longitude sets the clock, and matters most for the Moon, which moves its own width across the stars every hour.

Date and time

Drag the Sun around the ecliptic to change the date.
A sidereal day (23 h 56 min) is one turn of the sky. Stepping by sidereal days keeps every star in the same place, so only the Sun, Moon and planets move against the constellations. Turn on planet trails to see their paths, including the backward loops of retrograde motion.
Times are local mean solar time at your longitude (). At local noon the Sun is close to the meridian. "1 day/s" keeps the hour fixed, so you can watch the Sun move along the ecliptic and the Moon and planets wander among the stars.

Measure

Click anywhere on the sky to mark a point and read its altitude and azimuth. The mark stays on the sky as time runs.

About this app

This is the Celestial Sphere Explorer seen from where we really stand: at the center of the sphere, on the ground. The stars, constellations, Flamsteed's plates and deep-sky objects are the same data, from Hipparcos and the open d3-celestial data set by Olaf Frohn.

The sky turns once every sidereal day (23 h 56 m), and the whole sphere is precessed from the year 2000 to the date shown. The Sun, Moon and planets come from Paul Schlyter's orbital formulas, which agree with modern ephemerides to within about 2 arcminutes for the planets and 5 for the Moon. The Moon is placed as seen from your spot on Earth (its parallax is up to a degree). At wide views the Moon is drawn larger than life so its phase shows; zoom in to see it at its true size, about half a degree. Atmospheric refraction, which lifts objects near the horizon by about half a degree, is not included.

Stepping by sidereal days: each step is 23 h 56 m 4 s, one full turn of the sky, so the clock reads about 4 minutes earlier each step and the stars hold still. Over a year the Sun goes once around the ecliptic, the Moon about 13 times, and the outer planets trace their retrograde loops.

Azimuth is measured from north through east (north 0°, east 90°, south 180°, west 270°). Altitude is the angle above the horizon.
